# Course Architecture

Use this reference before deciding slide order or rewriting a deck whose flow feels abrupt.

## Start with a course contract

Write these items before drafting slides:

- **Learners:** prior knowledge, likely motivation, and language/technical barriers
- **Governing question:** the tension the whole session resolves
- **Transfer outcome:** what learners should be able to explain, judge, compare, or design afterward
- **Incoming question:** what the preceding session leaves unresolved
- **Outgoing question:** why the next session is necessary
- **Evidence constraints:** facts, cases, legal conditions, and uncertainties that must be preserved
- **Format constraints:** target duration, slide count, template, accessibility, and citation policy

Time and slide count constrain the design; they are not substitutes for the transfer outcome.

## Give each slide one educational function

Useful slide functions include:

- open a concrete experience or puzzle
- state the governing question
- explain a prerequisite concept
- show a historical or causal transition
- compare alternatives on an explicit dimension
- apply a framework to a case
- correct a likely misconception
- show evidence and teach how to interpret it
- turn evidence into a decision rule
- let the learner apply the rule
- recover the session's judgment tool

A slide title should normally express its claim or question. A topic-only title such as `AI 규제` gives the learner no reason to remember the slide.

## Build bridges before classifications

When a later slide requires a concept that has not been taught, do not hide the gap with a longer note. Add a short bridge slide or explain the concept before naming it. Typical missing bridges are:

- a classification before its dimensions are explained
- a legal obligation before the regulated subject and purpose are clear
- a chart before the axes, denominator, or measurement unit are introduced
- a company case before the operational problem is stated
- a process map before the difference between task, decision, handoff, and exception is clear

Conversely, delete a slide when it repeats an earlier claim, adds a case without a new inference, or exists only to defend the author's classification.

## A reusable narrative spine

Most lecture sessions can be adapted from this sequence:

1. **Experience or puzzle** — something the learner can recognize
2. **Question** — what the experience does not yet explain
3. **Necessary concepts** — only the vocabulary needed to reason
4. **Evidence or cases** — concrete observations, not an undifferentiated list
5. **Interpretation** — what the evidence does and does not support
6. **Judgment tool** — a reusable comparison, decision rule, or design principle
7. **Application** — a new case or question that requires the tool
8. **Recovery and continuation** — return to the opening question and expose the next unresolved issue

This is a reasoning sequence, not a mandatory number of slides.

## Topic-specific patterns

Use these as candidates, not fixed templates.

- **Technology history:** current behavioral change → learner question → earlier bottleneck → alternating breakthroughs and stagnation → current capability boundary → implications
- **Infrastructure and inequality:** lived experience → hidden physical bottleneck → access conditions → operating capability → observed gap → policy or organizational design
- **Regulation:** recognizable harm → common decision question → regulated categories → obligations and roles → comparison across regimes → action for an affected person or organization
- **Governance and coexistence:** conflict → stakeholder experience → historical accommodation → institutional adjustment → law/research/education combination → implementation choices
- **Organizational adoption:** everyday use → variation in depth → dimensions of use → cases → task selection → evidence and controls → task design
- **Process innovation:** lesson from earlier technology adoption → tool versus workflow → redesign principle → AI uncertainty and authority → discovery of actual work → allocation among rules/AI/people → controlled operating loop

## Transition design

Add an explicit transition when any of these changes:

- present ↔ history
- case ↔ principle
- technology ↔ organization or law
- physical infrastructure ↔ access conditions or operating capability
- task ↔ end-to-end process
- firm ↔ country or international cooperation
- evidence ↔ recommendation

A useful transition names both sides and the reason for moving:

> 지금까지는 장비가 얼마나 필요한지 살펴봤습니다. 장비가 있어도 실제 사용자가 접근하지 못하면 격차는 남기 때문에, 이제 이용 조건으로 넘어가겠습니다.

Avoid transitions that only announce a page change.

## Multi-session continuity

Maintain a small course map with one row per session:

| Session | Incoming question | Governing question | Judgment tool | Applied case | Outgoing question |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

At session boundaries, connect unresolved reasoning rather than merely naming the next title. The first session may summarize the whole course in one or two sentences; later sessions should state where they sit in that arc.

## Slide-plan schema

Before authoring, create one row per slide:

| No. | Educational function | Claim/question title | Required prerequisite | Evidence/example | Likely misconception | Why the next slide is needed |
|---:|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Use the schema to find missing bridges and redundant slides before spending time on layout.

## Opening and closing

An opening should introduce the instructor only as needed, pose the course question, and activate relevant experience. Do not read the cover text verbatim.

A closing should recover relationships, not list nouns. Give a short transfer question or design choice. Connect to the next session through a remaining problem rather than a generic preview.
